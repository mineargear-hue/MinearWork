Boiler Water Treatment 101 — What They Don't Teach You in Class

By Doug Minear Category: Boiler & Steam Read time: 8 min

Bad water treatment doesn't announce itself. It works slowly, quietly, and expensively — until the day it doesn't. Scale, corrosion, and carryover are the three ways untreated water destroys boiler systems. Here's what you actually need to know to stop all three.

Why Water Treatment Matters More Than You Think

A 1/16-inch scale deposit on a boiler heat transfer surface increases fuel consumption by approximately 12%. A 1/8-inch deposit — 25%. Scale is basically insulation in the wrong place. The heat that should be going into the water is going up the stack instead. You're burning fuel to heat limestone.

That's the economic argument. The safety argument is worse: localized overheating from scale deposits can lead to tube failures, and tube failures in high-pressure steam systems are not minor events.

The Three Enemies: Scale, Corrosion, and Carryover

Scale

Scale forms when dissolved minerals — primarily calcium and magnesium carbonates and sulfates — precipitate out of solution as water temperature rises. Hard water is scale waiting to happen. The fix is softening the makeup water before it enters the system, combined with internal chemical treatment to keep any remaining hardness in suspension.

Corrosion

Two main culprits: dissolved oxygen and low pH. Oxygen pitting is exactly what it sounds like — oxygen in the feedwater attacks metal surfaces and creates pits that eventually become holes. A deaerator is your primary weapon against oxygen. Mechanical deaeration removes most of it; chemical oxygen scavengers (sodium sulfite or DEHA) handle the rest. pH below 7 accelerates corrosion dramatically — keep your boiler water pH between 10.5 and 11.5 for steel boilers.

Carryover

Carryover is water droplets being carried into the steam system. It can be caused by high dissolved solids, high water level, foaming, or sudden load changes. Carryover hammers steam traps, control valves, and turbines. The fix is controlling dissolved solids through blowdown, maintaining proper water level, and using antifoam treatment when needed.

// Operational Warning

Water hammer from carryover sounds like someone hitting your steam lines with a sledgehammer. If you hear it, shut down and find the source before it finds a weak joint or valve body to fail through.

The Tests You Should Be Running Regularly

  • Hardness: Test makeup water and feedwater. Should be zero or near-zero after softening.
  • pH: Test boiler water. Target 10.5–11.5 for steel, 8.5–9.5 for copper-bearing systems.
  • Conductivity/TDS: High conductivity means high dissolved solids — time to blowdown.
  • Alkalinity (P and M): Indicates carbonate/bicarbonate levels and helps set blowdown frequency.
  • Sulfite (if using sodium sulfite): Maintain 30–60 ppm residual for oxygen scavenging.
  • Iron: Elevated iron indicates corrosion somewhere in the system.
// Testing Frequency

Daily testing is not excessive for an active boiler. Log every result with date, time, and who tested. This log is your defense when something goes wrong — and your early warning system when trends start moving the wrong direction.

Blowdown: Your Primary TDS Control

Surface blowdown removes dissolved solids from the water surface — where concentration is highest. Bottom blowdown removes sludge that has settled. Both are necessary and neither replaces the other.

Blowdown frequency should be based on your water test results, not a fixed schedule. A boiler with good makeup water quality may need less blowdown than one with poor source water. Test, measure, adjust.

Cycles of Concentration

Cycles of concentration (COC) tells you how much the dissolved solids have concentrated compared to your makeup water. If your makeup water has 100 ppm TDS and your boiler water has 300 ppm, you're running 3 cycles. Most low-pressure steam systems operate between 10–20 cycles. High-pressure systems run tighter — sometimes 3–5 cycles. Going too high means carryover risk; going too low means you're wasting water and treatment chemicals through excessive blowdown.

Working With Your Water Treatment Vendor

A good water treatment vendor is worth having. They should be doing periodic system surveys, reviewing your test logs, and adjusting your chemical program as conditions change. A bad vendor shows up once a year and sends you chemicals on autopilot. Know the difference. Ask questions. If your vendor can't explain why they're recommending what they're recommending, find one who can.

The Bottom Line

Water treatment is not glamorous. Nobody gets excited about conductivity readings. But the operator who understands their water chemistry and stays on top of it is the operator whose boiler runs clean, efficient, and doesn't surprise them at 2 AM. That's the whole job.
